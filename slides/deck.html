How do incumbents respond when a low-cost rival enters?

An agent-based model of DIGI’s entry into the Portuguese bundled-services market, built on the Consumer Choice model

Task 1

The decision rule: habit, exploration, utility

Consumers linked in a social network choose one of two products. Every tick, each one goes through three steps.

1. Habit

Keeps the product with probability equal to its satisfaction: log₁₀(1 + 9q)

2. Exploration

Otherwise, tries the other product with a small probability

3. Utility

Otherwise, picks the higher utility: own experience, friends’ information and the social norm

Outputs: market share of each product and clustering, the mean share of friends using the same product.

Baseline: 100 consumers, fixed population, the defaults of the original NetLogo model by Jager and Wang. This rule stays in every later version. With two products and the extensions switched off, the extended model reproduces the baseline exactly; an automatic test checks this.

Task 2

Baseline: strong clusters, unpredictable winner

1000 runs, tick 1000. Clustering 0.937 (95% CI 0.935–0.939). Orange share 0.506 on average, but SD 0.252: equal products, very different outcomes.

The mean market share is about one half, as expected with two equal products, but no single run looks like the mean: the middle 90% of runs lie between 0.11 and 0.92. Identical settings lead to different market outcomes, driven by social influence and chance. Horizon: clustering is still rising slowly, 0.948 at tick 2000.

Task 2

Baseline: what moves the outcome

Any norm creates clusters: clustering 0.543 at norm 0, about 0.94 from 0.1 up.

Small quality gaps decide: orange 0.951 when blue is 0.45, 0.071 when blue is 0.55.

Exploration breaks clusters: clustering falls to 0.673 at exploration 0.2.

100 runs per value, one parameter at a time. If asked: norm 0.1 and 0.2 give identical results. This is a real property of the rule with the same seeds, which we reproduced by rerunning the model; the likely reason is that consumers compare utilities, so a different weight need not change any choice.

Task 5

Learning: each incumbent runs its own bandit

Who and when

MEO, NOS and Vodafone, from DIGI’s entry, every 3 months. DIGI stays at €22.

Actions

List price × 1.00, 0.95, 0.90, 0.85 or 0.80, always above unit cost (× 0.60).

Reward

The margin of the last period, not revenue. Rivals’ prices are not observed.

Method

ε-greedy, ε = 0.1, step α = 0.2. Each price is tried once, in random order.

Compared with fixed-price scenarios, using the same random numbers: 30 runs per scenario, 10 years after entry.

Sutton and Barto 2018 for the method. Common random numbers mean seed s is used in every scenario, so differences are not due to chance draws; the bandit has its own generator for this reason. We corrected the original bandit: arms were tried in a fixed order from 0.95 to 0.80 while profit was falling, which made low prices look worse. This correction is decision D23.

Task 5

Learned prices cost little; cutting together costs a lot

Paired by seed, with 95% CI. DIGI share is the mean of the last 12 months. DIGI costs the incumbents about a tenth of their profit: no-entry is +11.4%.

Main bandit: minus 2.4 percent, confidence interval minus 3.5 to minus 1.3. All at 0.95 already costs minus 9.1 percent. A longer decision period makes the incumbents cut more, multiplier 0.97 at 6 months and 0.94 at 12 months, and DIGI grow less. Lower prices protect share but do not pay: list prices are the most profitable of the options where everybody moves together.

So list price is not an equilibrium, yet it is where the joint profit is highest among the symmetric prices we tested.

Each of the three confidence intervals excludes zero. Solo cuts take customers from the rivals, so joint profit still falls by 6.6 to 8.9 percent. A deeper solo cut, to 0.80, loses money for MEO, minus 11.1, and NOS, minus 8.3, and still pays for Vodafone, plus 13.1.

Task 5

Learned prices return to the list price

Mean multiplier 0.98. Across 9 ε, α combinations: joint profit −2.2% to −4.4%, multiplier 0.97–0.98. Not learned collusion: the bandit cannot see rivals, and its 3-month reward favours keeping margin.

The early dip is the trial phase, when every price is tried once. The remaining share of cuts in later years is mostly exploration: 10 percent of decisions are random; among non-exploring decisions the list price is chosen 95 percent of the time. Compatible with Calvano et al. 2020 in that independent algorithms stay above the competitive price, but the mechanism here is different: the short reward horizon. Contract customers arrive slowly, over 24-month contracts, while a price cut lowers margin immediately.

Task 5

DIGI grows slowly at first, then keeps gaining

Mean over the last 12 months: 22.6% at list prices, 18.1% with learned prices, 7.9% with all at ×0.90. Use these to compare scenarios: 10 years is far beyond our 30 months of data, so they are not forecasts.

Slow start, as in the data: contracts, habit and limited information hold consumers back. Without the entry, NOWO shrinks to about 1 percent. The dashed vertical line is the entry, month 12, November 2024.

Task 6

Both conclusions hold in all 9 changes

A solo cut pays for NOS (+9.8% to +38.7%) and Vodafone (+22.3% to +64.4%); only MEO at κ = 0.30 loses (−3.0%). Learned prices: multiplier 0.97–0.98, joint profit −0.1% to −4.1%.

One change at a time, not recalibrated, 20 runs per scenario, 95 percent confidence intervals. Because of the 20 runs the base row differs slightly from the 30-run results. With exits of 1 percent and margin 60 percent the joint-profit interval includes zero. Without recalibration, DIGI’s share at month 28 moves away from the data in some variants: 4.85 percent at kappa 0.10, 2.24 percent at kappa 0.30, so this tests the robustness of the conclusions, not the fit.

Conclusions

Contracts and habit slow a much cheaper rival

The calibrated model reaches ANACOM’s 3.2% at month 28 at half the price.

Matching on price does not pay

All at ×0.90 stops much of DIGI’s growth, but costs 17.5% of joint profit.

Each incumbent is tempted to cut alone

A solo cut gains 14.9% to 36.2%: a prisoner’s dilemma.

Simple learners stay near list price without communicating

Consistent with what we observe: main brands kept prices, and the incumbents answered with low-cost brands.

The last point is a match to a qualitative fact, not a test: the model has no low-cost second brands, and the result comes from short-horizon rewards, not from a learned collusive strategy.

Limitations

LimitationWhat it means
Long horizon10 years against 30 months of data: compare scenarios, do not forecast
Low mobility at contract endUnder 1% switch in the model; Confraria et al. estimate 13.6% (mobile, 2014)
Drift without DIGIMEO 41.1% to 39.7% and NOWO 2.4% to 1.5%, months 12–72
Bandit designNo view of rivals and a short reward horizon; no second low-cost brands
Assumed parametersκ, the initial contract share and the commitment spread have no direct data
All scenarios are compared with the same random numbers, which limits the effect of the drift on the comparisons. Natural next steps: a learner that observes rivals, longer reward horizons and low-cost brands as an action.
